Assessment objectives — AO3 Listening

The four things examiners are actually testing

Cambridge writes every listening question to test one of four skills, L1–L4. Knowing which one a question targets tells you what kind of answer to expect.

L1 · easiest

Specific information

Wording: demonstrate understanding of specific information.

Sounds like: “The tour leaves at 9:15.” “It costs £14.” A fact is stated plainly. You match it, or you do not.

L2 · medium

Speakers' ideas, opinions, attitudes

Wording: demonstrate understanding of speakers' ideas, opinions and attitudes.

Sounds like: “Honestly, I think it was a waste of money.” The opinion is stated, but in evaluative language.

L3 · medium-hard

Connections between ideas

Wording: demonstrate understanding of the connections between ideas, opinions and attitudes.

Sounds like: “Because the buses stopped running, she ended up walking.” You must chain cause to effect across sentences.

L4 · hardest

What is implied, not stated

Wording: demonstrate understanding of what is implied but not directly stated.

Sounds like: “Well… I suppose the seats were comfortable.” The verdict is never spoken — you infer it from tone and hedging.

Use this to calibrate effort. If an option is a word-for-word copy of something you heard, it is usually an L1 trap. If an option restates the same idea in different words, it is usually the real answer. L4 questions are deliberately designed so that the correct option is never in the audio.
3 minThis is the intellectual spine of the whole workshop. The syllabus spells these out as AO3: L1 specific information, L2 speakers' ideas/opinions/attitudes, L3 connections between ideas, L4 implied meaning. Exam-day mechanics

What actually happens in the room

Most lost marks in listening are lost before the first extract — through poor preparation of the room, the paper or the answer sheet.

🔊The audio

Delivered by the centre, checked in advance. If you cannot hear clearly, raise your hand immediately — do not sit through a whole exercise guessing. Reporting a problem during the test is what lets the centre act on it.

📄The question paper

You write your answers on the question paper first. This is a working document — underline, circle, strike through. Nobody marks the question paper.

🖊️The answer sheet

A separate multiple-choice sheet. Losing the sheet, or filling it in at the wrong line, can cost an entire exercise. This is the classic silent disaster.

Allowed: working on the question paper, writing in pencil, changing answers, using the 6-minute transfer to double-check.

Not allowed: phones, discussing answers, or writing after the recording ends and the 6 minutes' transfer time has finished. The syllabus states that dictionaries may not be used in Components 1 and 3 — assume the same here and do not rely on one.
The three silent killers — none of them about English:
  • Off-by-one transfer — the answers are right, the letters are on the wrong lines.
  • Blank answers — leaving a question empty. A blank scores zero with certainty; a guess sometimes scores one.
  • Unprepared silence — sitting passively during the reading time instead of predicting.
One-in-four is free. In Exercise 1 with four options, a blind guess is worth 0.25 of a mark on average. Leave eight lines blank across the paper and you have handed back roughly two marks — for nothing. (Cambridge does not deduct marks for wrong answers on multiple-choice papers; check the current Cambridge Handbook for the exact wording.)
3 minThis slide exists because three of the most common mark losses are nothing to do with English. Walk the left column as logistics, then spend real time on the right column. Emphasise: write on the question paper (it is your scrap paper and it is never marked), and the answer sheet is a separate risk. Teach the off-by-one failure explicitly and give the fix now: transfer in blocks of five, counting lines aloud in your head, and cover the rest of the sheet with your hand so your eye cannot drift. On guessing: there is no negative marking in Cambridge IGCSE, so every question must have a letter. Illustrate the expected value: in Exercise 1 each question has four options, so a blind guess is worth 0.25 of a mark on average — leave eight blank lines and you have given away about two marks for nothing. Across the paper that can be the difference between grades. Remind them of the invigilator script: if the audio is unclear or the equipment fails, they must raise a hand at once — centres can request special consideration only if the problem is reported during the test.

The universal method

Every question, in four beats: the T.A.C.O. loop

One routine you can run on all 40 questions, in all five exercises, without thinking. Learn it until it is automatic.

T
Target the question — before the audio starts Read the question and every option. Underline the key noun and the question word. Decide what kind of answer it wants: a place? a time? a feeling? Free marks happen here.
silence

Why it works. It gives the same job to every hearing. If you always do the same thing, you stop spending your working memory on “what should I be doing now?” and spend it on English.
The most common failure is skipping T. Candidates start listening with unread options, hear a familiar word, and lock it in. That is exactly how the paper is designed to catch you.
Never do this on hearing 2: re-reading all four options from scratch. You do not have time between extracts, and you will miss the opening words of the text.
Make it visible. On your question paper it should physically look like this: T underlined keywords · A pencil dash beside a candidate ⟶ C circled final letter · O letter transferred.
4 minThis is the single most important slide in the deck. Teach T.A.C.O. as a routine, not a suggestion — the point is that it removes decisions, and decisions cost time and working memory. Run it physically: tell students to hold up their pen and show you what "A" looks like on a page (a small pencil dash, not a circle) versus "C/O" (a firm circle). If it is not visually distinguishable they will not know whether they have committed. Land the two warnings: (1) skipping T is the number-one cause of trap answers, because unread options plus a familiar word equals a confident wrong answer; (2) on hearing 2, never re-read everything — narrow to confirming your choice and interrogating the nearest distractor. Every exercise slide that follows applies this loop, so use it as a refrain: "What is T here? What is A here?" By Exercise 5 they should be answering before you ask.
The two hearings

Hearing 1 and hearing 2 are two different jobs

Students who score 20/40 usually use both hearings for the same task. Students who score 35+/40 use them for different tasks.

Hearing 1Hearing 2

1️⃣The 1st hearing is your map

You are building a mental model: who, where, why, and in what order the answers arrive. Mark territory, do not plant a flag.

2️⃣The 2nd hearing is your judgment

Now listen for the exact words that decide between two options — often a single word: not, only, already, instead, unless.

🚨Between the two: the 8-second gap

Each extract is followed by a pause. Use it: commit your dash to a letter, peek at the next question, breathe.

3 minThis slide is the highest-leverage behavioural change in the workshop. Most mid-band candidates use hearing 1 to answer and hearing 2 to check they were right — which means they never actually use hearing 2 analytically. Teach the split explicitly: hearing 1 = build the map, mark territory; hearing 2 = listen narrowly for the deciding word, plant the flag. The deciding-word list is worth writing on the board: not, never, only, already, instead, rather, unless, but, actually, the problem was, in the end, at first. These words are where Cambridge reverses meaning, and they are almost always the hinge between the trap and the answer. Also give them the inter-hearing ritual: when the recording stops, do not relax — finalise the letter, then look at the next question. That 8-second gap is where a disciplined candidate steals a mark from a passive one.
Step T in practice

The 20 seconds that are worth 5 marks

Before every extract you get a short silence. It is not a rest — it is the only time in the exam when you control the information.

1Read the question → 2Underline the key noun → 3Underline the wh-word
4Name the answer type → 5Predict 2–3 words → 6Spot the odd option out
Then stop and look up. The scan is finished when you know what you are listening for. Continuing to stare at the paper while the audio plays is how answers are missed.
Question 3. What will the weather be like at the weekend?
A sunny B rainy C snowy D windy
Key noun: weather at the weekend
Wh-word: What… like → the answer is a condition, not a time or a place
Answer type: one of four weather conditions — so I am listening for ONE adjective
Predict: rain, sun, cold, wind, cloud, storm, mild
Odd one out: all four are weather — there is no easy elimination here, so the intensity will decide it

Do

  • Predict the type of answer, not the exact answer.
  • Notice words that will be said differently: weekend → Saturday, Sunday.
  • Read all options before the audio starts — a trap lives in the option you skipped.

Don't

  • Don't read ahead to the next exercise — you will miss this one.
  • Don't lock in an option just because it is the first one you read.
  • Don't keep your head down once the audio begins.
4 minDeliver the six-step scan as a physical drill. Put a question on the board and read it aloud with the class doing steps 2–6 out loud, so they hear what a scan sounds like in the head. The worked example is deliberately about weather because it exposes the hardest part of pre-listening: when all four options belong to the same category, prediction cannot eliminate anything — so you must predict the dimension instead (intensity, timing, degree). That is a genuine exam skill, not a trick. Stress the "then stop and look up" instruction. The most common error is a candidate who keeps reading during the extract and misses the first five words — and the first five words are usually where the topic and the speaker's attitude are set. DON'T read ahead: the paper is sequential. There is no strategic advantage to skipping forward, and there is a large cost.

Exercise 1 — technique

Read the pictures like a detective, not a tourist

Four images that look unrelated are actually four versions of the same decision. Your job is to find the exact thing that separates them.

1
Name each picture in one word Force a label: “clock”, “bus”, “umbrella”, “guitar”. Naming stops the picture from being decoration and turns it into data.
2
Find the axis of difference Ask: what single dimension changes across A, B, C, D? Time? Place? Activity? Quantity? That dimension is the question.
3
Predict the words for that axis If the axis is time: o'clock, half past, quarter to, delayed, brought forward, in the morning. Pre-load the vocabulary so the audio confirms rather than surprises.
4
Watch for the pair that look identical When two options are near-duplicates — 9:00 and 9:30, or “library” and “bookshop” — the answer is one of those two. That is where Cambridge places the discrimination.
Worked thinking — “What time does the shop open?”
A 8:00 · B 9:00 · C 9:30 · D 10:00

Axis of difference = time. Near-duplicates = B and C. So the audio will almost certainly mention both 9:00 and 9:30, and one of them will be rejected (“we used to open at nine, but not any more”). Your whole job is to catch the rejection.
The one-word naming rule is not cosmetic. Students who “look at” pictures skim them and then listen vaguely. Students who label them know exactly which of four facts they are waiting for.
Beware the visible-but-unmentioned picture. Every option is designed to be plausible. Do not assume that because a picture appears, the audio will mention it — some options exist only to be the opposite of what is said.
4 minThis is the technique slide for Exercise 1 and it generalises to any visual-option task. The core idea to repeat three times: four pictures are not four ideas, they are one idea at four settings. Have students practise the "axis of difference" out loud. Give them a set — e.g. four images of a bus, a train, a bicycle and walking — and ask what the axis is (mode of transport). Then a set of four clocks (time). Then four faces (emotion). Then teach the near-duplicate heuristic. It is genuinely reliable: when two of the four options are almost the same, the item's discrimination lies between them, because otherwise the option would not exist. In the worked example on the next slide, see whether the class can predict which two options are the real contest. Finally, pre-loading vocabulary is the highest-value pre-listening action. If the four pictures are times, the words half past / quarter to / brought forward / delayed / sharp should be primed before the audio. That is the difference between recognition and confusion.

Exercise 1 — the traps

The four traps Exercise 1 is built from

Extract after extract, the same four patterns recur. Name them once and you will start hearing them arrive.

Trap 1

The number that gets cancelled

A time, price or quantity is stated clearly — then withdrawn.

Markers: not · it was… but · we used to · actually · sorry, I mean

Fix: wait for the sentence to end before you decide.

Trap 2

The past plan

The original arrangement is described in detail — but it is no longer true.

Markers: was going to · were supposed to · had planned to · we'd hoped

Fix: listen for what happens after the “but”.

Trap 3

The mentioned object

The picture is named, but as background or as a negative: “the bus was late, so I walked.”

Markers: because · so instead · I didn't · rather than

Fix: ask who is doing what, finally?

Trap 4

The near-identical pair

Two options differ by a small detail — 15 minutes, one stop, £2. Both get spoken.

Markers: nothing special — this is precision listening.

Fix: write both candidate numbers on the paper beside the question.

Exercise 1 in one sentence: the answer is what is true at the end of the extract, not what is said during it. Every trap above is a version of that single idea.
4 minName the traps and then have students hunt them. The four are: (1) the cancelled number, (2) the past plan, (3) the mentioned object, (4) the near-identical pair. The unifying rule is at the bottom and it is worth repeating loudly: what is true at the end of the extract. Exercise 1 extracts are almost always mini-narratives with a correction inside them. Teach the markers as listening signposts, not vocabulary. was going to, were supposed to, supposedly, I know that's what I said, but… — when students hear these, their pen should pause. For trap 4, give the practical habit: write the candidate numbers side by side on the question paper (e.g. "9 / 30?" ) so the decision is visual rather than remembered. Working memory is the bottleneck in listening, and offloading to paper is free.

Exercise 2 — technique

The two questions are usually doing different jobs

One tends to be answerable from a stated fact. The other tends to need a stance: how someone feels, what they mean, what they imply.

Question A · often literal

“Which…? When…? How much…? Where…?”

A detail that is stated. Answerable from hearing 1 if you scanned properly.

Your action: underline the noun, then listen for the rejection words — the fact you want will be stated and then partly withdrawn.

Question B · often interpretive

“Why…? How does she feel…? What does he mean…? What is her opinion of…?”

A stance, reason or implication. Usually not stated in those words.

Your action: listen for tone and hedges, not content words — to be honest, I suppose, if anything, I'd rather, it's not that… it's just…

This is a pattern, not a published rule. Cambridge does not promise one literal and one interpretive question per extract. Treat it as a strong tendency that shapes where you place your attention — and stay ready for two literal or two interpretive questions.
1Read both questions before the audio — never just the first 2Label each: F (fact) or S (stance) 3On hearing 1, secure the F question 4On hearing 2, lock the S question — you need the second hearing for it 5Check that your two answers are consistent with each other
Consistency check — a free mark. If the speaker is clearly delighted with something, an answer of “He was disappointed” elsewhere in the pair is almost certainly wrong. Your two answers about one speaker should tell a coherent story.
Why write F and S on the paper? Because it converts a vague instruction (“read the questions”) into a visible checklist. You can see, at a glance during the audio, which question is still open.
4 minTeach the F/S labelling habit explicitly, and start with the honest caveat on the slide: this is a pattern, not a rule. Cambridge does not guarantee one literal and one interpretive question per extract. Students who are told it is a rule get caught when it is not. What IS reliable is the underlying skill distinction: some questions are answered by a stated fact, some by a stance or reason. Teaching students to notice which one they are facing is the point. The consistency check is the highest-value trick here: two answers about the same speaker should be psychologically coherent. If one says "delighted" and the other says "disappointed", at least one is wrong — and students can often fix it without re-listening. Syllabus confirmation: Exercise 2 has three written options which "may be words or short phrases", and the questions "test understanding of details, opinions, ideas and implied information expressed by the speaker(s)".
Exercise 2 — the golden rule

The correct option paraphrases. The distractors quote.

This is the single most useful generalisation in the whole listening paper. It comes straight out of listening-assessment research.

The design rule (Buck 2001; Field 2003, 2008): test writers make the key a paraphrase of the audio, and make the distractors re-use the audio's own words. Recognising your own vocabulary is rewarded by feeling confident — which is precisely what the trap is for.
SPEAKER: I've completely given up trying to persuade him — you can talk to him for hours and he still won't change.
AShe no longer tries to change his mind.✓ Paraphrase of “given up trying to persuade him”. Zero shared content words.
BShe talks to him for hours.✕ Verbatim audio words — and a detail, not the point.
CShe wants him to be persuaded.✕ Recycles “persuade”; reverses the meaning — she has stopped.
The operating habit: when you hear an option's exact words in the audio, become suspicious, not confident. The familiar word is bait.
But do not invert it blindly: a verbatim match is sometimes the answer — for L1 detail questions Cambridge can be literal. Use this rule to check, not to decide alone.
4 minThis slide is research-backed and it changes scores. The finding, from Buck's Assessing Listening and Field's work on lexical segmentation (Field 2003, ELT Journal 57/4), is that the key paraphrases while distractors recycle audio lexis — so familiarity with a word predicts the wrong answer more often than the right one. Teach the emotional flip: recognition currently makes students feel safe. We want recognition to make them suspicious. Say the phrase out loud: "if the option says exactly what I heard, I should check it, not tick it." Then immediately teach the caveat on the bottom right, because an over-corrected student starts rejecting correct verbatim answers. For pure L1 detail questions, Cambridge can be literal. So the rule is a check, not the decision. The drill: after any Exercise 2 practice, have students draw a line from each option to the words in the transcript that licensed or killed it. That mapping exercise is what turns the rule into a reflex.
Exercise 2 — implied meaning (L4)

When the answer is never said, only meant

L4 is 24 of the 40 marks across the paper. Here is how to hear something that is not there.

Signal 1

Hedging

“I suppose it was fine.” “It wasn't terrible.” “It could have been worse.”
Repeated softeners usually mean mild dissatisfaction, not approval.

Signal 2

Contrast

“The location was perfect — although the price…”
Whatever follows although, but, having said that, that said is usually the real verdict.

Signal 3

Understatement & irony

“Well, that was an interesting evening.” With a flat tone, “interesting” means the opposite.

Signal 4

Answering a question with a question

“Do you think it's worth it?” — “It's certainly cheap…” The speaker is implying cheap is the only good thing about it.

Signal 5

What is conspicuously missing

If someone lists the good points and then stops, the omission is the message. “The food was great, the staff were lovely…” and nothing else — the room was probably awful.

The L4 method — three questions:
1. What did the speaker choose to say?
2. What did they avoid saying?
3. Which option would a polite but unenthusiastic person choose?
Never choose the option that is true about the world — only about this text. “Plausible real-world” options are a documented distractor type (Buck 2001). You are being tested on what these speakers said, not on what is generally true.
5 minThis is the L4 teaching slide, and it is the highest-value conceptual content in the deck — 24 of 40 marks are in exercises that include L4. Name the five signals and demonstrate each with your voice. Hedging in particular: say "I suppose it was fine" flatly and ask the class whether the person liked it. They will hear the dissatisfaction — the point is to make them trust that instinct rather than hunting for a stated opinion. The three-question method is the transferable procedure: what did they say, what did they avoid, what would a polite-but-unenthusiastic person choose. That last question is remarkably effective on real items. Then land the world-knowledge warning. It is a documented distractor type in the literature (Buck 2001, Assessing Listening): options that are true of the world rather than the text. Students must answer "what did these speakers say", full stop. Diagnostic note worth keeping in your head — Goh (2000) classified listening failures into perception, parsing and utilisation, and L4 failures are almost always utilisation failures: the student understood every word but missed the intent.

🎤One speaker, no interruptions

A talk or presentation — one voice for the whole extract. There is no second speaker to confuse you, and no dialogue to follow.

📝You get the sentences

Eight incomplete sentences that summarise the talk. Each has a gap, and three written options (a word or short phrase) to fill it.

🧩The gap is a clue, not a gap

Because you can see the sentence around the gap, you can use grammar, collocation and word class to narrow three options to one before the audio begins.

Two gifts in this exercise. First, the sentences normally follow the order of the talk — in practice they act as a map of the monologue's structure (check the rubric and the items on the day rather than assuming it). Second, the options must fit into a written sentence, so grammar alone sometimes identifies the answer. No other exercise gives you either.
The one thing Exercise 3 never tests: implied meaning (L4). The syllabus lists L1, L2 and L3 only. So do not hunt for hidden meanings here — listen for information and main ideas, stated plainly.

Exercise 3 — technique

Read the gap like a grammar question first

Before the audio starts, you can often reduce three options to one without knowing anything about the topic.

1
What word class does the gap need? A noun? A verb form? An adjective? A number? Circle the gap and write the class above it. Now read each option and cross out anything of the wrong class.
2
What does the grammar force? “The garden was built on land that used to be a ____” → the gap must be a singular countable noun. Plural options and verb options die immediately. Grammar has just done work for you, for free.
3
Does the sentence have a polarity? “The biggest problem was ____” needs something negative. An option that is obviously positive is wrong before the audio starts.
4
Which options collocate with the sentence? English has strong word partnerships. If the sentence says “raised a lot of ____”, the option is far more likely to be money than weather.
Worked prediction — no audio needed
The site they built the garden on used to be a ______ .
Acar park✓ singular countable noun — fits
Bparking✕ uncountable; “a parking” is ungrammatical
Ccars parked✕ clause, not a noun phrase
You have already improved your odds from 1-in-3 to 1-in-1. Now the audio only has to confirm your prediction instead of doing all the work.
Careful: some items test a word or phrase rather than grammar — e.g. “The speaker began the project because she ____”. Here the gap needs a verb phrase, and the options will all be grammatically possible. When grammar cannot separate them, fall back to meaning and to the order of the talk.
4 minExercise 3 is the only place in the paper where a candidate can do real elimination work before the audio — so teach it properly. Walk the four steps with the class on the worked prediction. The critical insight is that the gap imposes a word class, and word class alone can kill an option. "A ____" cannot take "parking" or "cars parked". That is a free elimination. Steps 3 and 4 extend the same logic to polarity and collocation — a problem-sentence wants something negative; strong collocations narrow the field further. Then be honest about the limit: when the options are all the same word class (three different verb phrases, say), grammar cannot decide, and you fall back on meaning and the order of the talk. Teach the ceiling of this technique so students do not over-trust it and reject a correct answer because "the grammar felt odd". Syllabus confirms each option is "either a word or a short phrase" — which is precisely why word-class analysis works so often.
Exercise 3 — during the audio

Margin-map the talk instead of writing words

You are not taking notes for an essay. You need a skeleton — and you need it in about twelve words.

What you write in the margin — hearing 1:
1 why / problem 2 + 3 − 4 but… 5 result 6 2019 7 opinion? 8 future
What that map tells you. Item 2 was positive, item 3 was negative, item 4 turned on a contrast, item 7 was an opinion. Now, on hearing 2, you know exactly what to listen for — and you know which of your answers to check hardest.
Write numbers and single symbols — never words. Every full word you write is a second your ear is not listening. Letters and symbols cost nothing.

🔢One number per item

Before the audio, number your margin 1–8 next to the eight sentences. That is your grid.

➕Symbols beat words

+ positive · − negative · ? unsure · X ruled out · → cause/effect · BUT contrast · # number or date heard

🧭Track the signposts

Monologues are built in stages. Mark where the speaker moves on: first of all, then, the next thing, after that, now, finally. Those markers tell you when to move to the next sentence.

If you fall behind, do not catch up on the last item — jump forward. Find the next question number, and rejoin there. An item you have abandoned is already lost; the next one is not.
4 minThis is the note-taking slide, and the discipline is the opposite of what students expect. They want to write words; we want them to write signals. Demonstrate the margin map on a real monologue: play 30 seconds of any authentic talk and have the class write only numbers and symbols in the margin beside items 1–8. They will be surprised how much they recapture. The two rules: one number per item, and symbols instead of words. Every word written while listening is a second of hearing given away. Teach the signpost vocabulary as a navigation tool, not as vocabulary to learn — the student who hears "the next thing was" knows to move his eyes down one sentence number. The recovery rule at the bottom matters enormously: if you lose an item, do not claw it back. Redirect to the next question number. Chasing a lost item usually costs the following two. This is where candidates bleed marks in Exercise 3.
Exercise 3 — the traps

Three options, and two of them were built for you

Because the answer must fit a sentence, Exercise 3 traps are unusually predictable.

Trap 1

The plausible-but-unsaid

An option that fits the sentence perfectly and is completely reasonable — and is simply never mentioned.

Fix: every option needs evidence from the audio. If you cannot point to the moment, you are guessing.

Trap 2

The mentioned-and-rejected

An option that IS said — attached to something else. “Everyone said the hard part would be the weather. Actually, the paperwork took nine months.”

Fix: hunt for actually · in fact · turned out · the real problem was.

Trap 3

The wrong-relationship

The option names something from the text but attaches it to the wrong thing — wrong person, wrong time, wrong reason, wrong place.

Fix: ask who? and when? before you accept a match.

The evidence rule. Three options, one mark, and a written sentence to complete. The correct option is the one you can justify with a specific phrase from the audio. If you cannot say the phrase, you have not earned the mark — you have gambled at 1-in-3.
Why "it fits the sentence" is not enough. Trap 1 exists precisely because a sentence can be completed sensibly in at least two ways. Plausibility is what the trap is made of. Only the text decides.
3 minAll three Exercise 3 traps share one root: the option has to be plausible to be worth writing. So plausibility is not evidence. Say that sentence twice. Trap 1, plausible-but-unsaid: fits the gap grammatically and semantically, never appears in the audio. This is the most common wrong answer for capable students, because capable students reason their way to a sensible completion instead of listening for the text. Trap 2, mentioned-and-rejected: the option is in the audio, attached to a rejected idea. The fix is the contrast-marker list: actually, in fact, it turned out, the real problem was, in the end. Underline these in the transcript during practice until they jump out. Trap 3, wrong relationship: right content, wrong attachment — the number belongs to a different year, the reason belongs to a different person. The reflective questions are simply who and when. Land the evidence rule: they should be able to quote the phrase that earns each mark. Train the habit by making them do it in feedback — no quote, no mark.

👥Six separate short monologues

Six different people, usually on one shared topic — six opinions on the same thing. Each speaker is short, and each is played twice.

🗂️You match, you do not choose

There is no A/B/C in the question. You pick the statement (A–H) that best fits each speaker, and you record it against that speaker.

♻️The marking logic matters

Eight statements, six speakers. If statements may be used at most once — check the rubric on the day — then two statements are left over and none are repeated.

Why this exercise is the hardest to keep straight. Six speakers and eight statements is fourteen pieces of information to hold, while the audio keeps moving. Without a written system you will assign the same statement twice and not notice. The system is the skill here.
And the worst value on the paper. Six marks for the most complex task — versus ten marks for Exercise 2. If you are running out of composure, do not sacrifice a calmer exercise to rescue this one.

Exercise 4 — technique

Reduce all eight statements to three words each

You cannot hold eight sentences in your head while listening. You can hold eight three-word labels.

1
Read all eight statements before the first speaker Yes, all eight. This is the one exercise where reading ahead is the technique, because the statements are the answer bank.
2

Exercise 5 — technique

Attribution: the skill that decides Exercise 5

Every question is really asking the same hidden question: who said this, and did they mean it?

1
Fix the two voices before they speak Note the timbre. The interviewer is usually the higher, more clipped voice reading prepared questions; the interviewee is the one who explains, hesitates and tells stories.
2
Tag every opinion as you hear it In your head, prefix each evaluative statement with “INT says…” or “MAIN says…”. It costs nothing and it removes the whole wrong-speaker category.
3
Distrust anything the interviewer states as fact INT: “So it must have been terrifying?” — that is a guess, not a fact. Wait for the main speaker to agree or correct. If they correct, the correction is the answer.
4
Watch the interviewer's summary traps INT: “So the money was the main thing.” MAIN: “Not exactly — I'd have done it anyway.” The interviewer's summary is a ready-made distractor. The rebuttal is the answer.
INT: So it was all about the charity, then?
MAIN: People assume that — and yes, we raised a lot. But honestly, I'd had a hard year and I wanted to prove I could finish something. That was the real reason.
What a question would do here: “Why did she do the walk?”
A the charity — the interviewer's guess, echoed by the main speaker as an assumption → trap.
B to prove she could finish something — the main speaker's “real reason” → answer.
Note the double bluff: the trap is true (they did raise a lot) and the answer is modest (“prove I could finish something”). Real reasons are usually the quieter ones — introduced by honestly, really, actually, the real reason is.
Never answer from the interviewer's sentence. It is a question paper artefact, not evidence. If you can only cite the interviewer, you have not found the answer.

Four ways Exercise 5 takes a mark off you

Three of the four are attribution failures. All four are avoidable with a habit you already have.

Trap 1 · the loudest

Correct content, wrong speaker

The option is exactly what the interviewer said — often in a leading question.

Trap 2

The interviewer's paraphrase

The interviewer summarises, the main speaker corrects. The option matches the summary, not the correction.

Degree shift

The option turns a mild view into a strong one, or the reverse: “it wasn't ideal” becomes “she was furious”.

Defence: match the intensity, not just the direction. A negative option is only right if the speech was negative to that degree.

Trap 4

True of life in general, false of this interview. Often involves what “experts say” or what “anyone would feel”.

Defence: you are tested on what these two people said. Nothing else counts.

Why you lose marks: three different failures

“I didn't understand” hides three completely different problems — and each has a different cure. Diagnose yours before you practise more.

Level 1 · PERCEPTION

“I did not even hear the word.”

What it looks like: a word you know perfectly becomes an unrecognisable blur; you cannot find where one word ends and the next begins; you miss the very first words.

The real cause: connected speech — weak forms, linking, elision. Not vocabulary.

The cure: micro-listening transcription — 5–10 minutes, 2–3× a week.

Level 2 · PARSING

“I heard every word and forgot it instantly.”

What it looks like: you understand each sentence as it arrives, then cannot recall the argument; you lose the thread by item 4.

The real cause: no structure in working memory. You never built a skeleton.

The cure: margin-mapping — numbers and symbols, never prose. And never translate word-by-word in your head.

Level 3 · UTILISATION

“I understood it all — and still chose wrong.”

What it looks like: the words were clear, the meaning was clear, and the option you chose was the trap. Or you got the message but missed the intent.

The real cause: you answered the wrong question, or missed the stance.

The cure: question-word discipline and the trap taxonomy.

Diagnose before you drill. Track your errors across three practice papers and label each one 1, 2 or 3. If most are level 1, stop doing more mock papers — your time belongs in micro-listening. If most are level 3, your English is fine and your technique is the problem. Most B2-high/C1 students lose marks at level 3, not level 1.
The hidden habit: mental translation. Translating each phrase into your first language consumes the exact working memory you need for the next sentence. It feels thorough; it is the most expensive habit in the room. Practise listening for meaning, not for word-for-word equivalents.

Numbers, times, dates and prices

Numbers are the most trap-rich content in the paper, because they can be said twice and both times can be true of something different.

🔁Self-repair

“It's three fifteen — sorry, three fifty.” The first number is the trap. Wait for the full sentence, and always let a correction finish.

👥Two-referent numbers

“The coat was £40 and the scarf £15.” Three possible answers exist: 40, 15, 55. Match the number to its owner.

≈Approximate vs exact

“About two hundred” then “two hundred and eighteen”. If the question asks how many exactly, only the precise figure answers it.

÷Unit and scope switch

per person vs total, per night vs per week, £ vs €, per cent vs amount. The number is right; the unit is wrong.

🔉-teen and -ty

thirteen / thirty · fourteen / forty · fifteen / fifty · sixteen / sixty
Stress is the only difference. Drill these aloud until they are automatic — this trap appears again and again.

🔢Ordinal vs cardinal

“the third of May” vs “May the third” — and “the third attempt” vs “three attempts”. Listen for the word the and the noun after it.

🔤Spelled names & homophones

Phone letters (S for Sierra), plus confusables: B/V · G/J · S/F · M/N. Homophones: wait/weight · eight/ate · one/won · knew/new.

The three-minute drill. Twenty numbers dictated in three minutes: one self-repair, one two-referent pair, one -teen/-ty pair, one unit switch. Twice a week. This is the cheapest mark-recovery drill in the whole deck.

Tone carries meaning that words do not

The same sentence can mean approval or contempt. In L2 and L4 questions, intonation is evidence.

🎭Understatement

“Well… that was an interesting evening.” With a flat, slow delivery, interesting means the opposite. English speakers soften negative opinions constantly — and examination questions test whether you noticed.

🔀Contrastive stress

Stress decides the meaning:
“I didn't say that.” → someone else did.
“I didn't say that.” → I meant something else.
“I didn't say it.” → I implied it.

📉Trailing off

“The food was lovely, the staff were great…” and then nothing. The sentence that was never finished carries the verdict. Listen for the omission.

🎚️Read the three dials

1. Speed — hesitation and slowing mean discomfort or doubt.
2. Pitch — a rise on an apparently positive word can signal sarcasm.
3. Stress — the emphasised word is the speaker's real focus.

When the words and the delivery disagree, trust the delivery.

How this wins marks. An L4 option like “She was less impressed than she expected” is usually supported by one hedged phrase and one flat tone, not by a statement. If you only process words, you cannot answer it.
Do not over-read tone. If a question is purely factual (L1), ignore delivery entirely and answer from the content. Tone is for L2 and L4 — do not let it talk you out of a clear factual answer.

The inference ladder: L1 up to L4

The four assessment objectives are a ladder, not four separate skills. Climb it deliberately on every extract — and know when to stop.

L4 · impliedYou assemble the answer from evidence. “Physically I was fine, but I missed people and it was hard.” → lonely. The word is never said.
Ask: what would I conclude if I were in the room?
L3 · connectionsYou link two ideas. “Because the buses stopped, she walked.” → the reason she walked.
Ask: cause → effect, or earlier → later? Which direction?
L2 · stanceYou identify an opinion. “Honestly, it was a waste of money.” → she regrets it.
Ask: positive or negative, and how strongly?
L1 · stated factYou match information. “The tour leaves at nine fifteen.” → time = 9:15.
Ask: was it stated, and was it later changed?
Climb every time. A secure habit: after each extract, ask yourself the four questions from bottom to top. Items are not evenly distributed — 24 of the 40 marks involve L4 — but the ladder keeps you from stopping at L1 by default.
Do not over-climb. Exercise 3 tests only L1–L3, and many items elsewhere are honestly just L1. Inventing an implication where none exists is as expensive as missing one. The question and the option wording tell you how high to go.
How to know a question is L4: usually the option wording is a feeling, judgement or intention — “disappointed”, “keen to return”, “unsure”, “wanted to prove something” — rather than a fact. Feeling words in options are your L4 alarm.

1

2
First listen — no stopping, no rewinding Full exam conditions for this listen, even in practice. Mark your confidence with ✓ or ? beside each answer.
3
Second listen — find where it broke Before marking, replay and note the exact moment you lost each question. Not the answer — the moment. This is the diagnostic gold.
4
Analyse — with the transcript For each error: what phrase licensed the right answer? Name the trap type (1–9) and the breakdown level (perception / parsing / utilisation). Add two entries to your paraphrase journal.
5

The metacognitive cycle forces you to compare your prediction with the reality, locate the breakdown, and name the cause — and it is the step that has been shown to produce measurable improvement over a term (Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari 2010).
The temptation at step 4: writing “careless”. “Careless” is not diagnosable and therefore cannot be fixed. Every error must be labelled with a trap number or a breakdown level, or it will simply repeat.
The one-goal rule at step 5 is not soft. Five goals is the same as no goals — nothing gets attention. One goal per paper, written at the top of the next paper, is how a specific listening habit is installed in about three weeks.
Do this with three papers and you will know your own profile: which exercises cost you marks, which trap types recur, and whether your problem is decoding, structure or interpretation. That knowledge is worth more than another ten mock papers.

The research finding is important and worth stating: cycle-trained groups significantly outperformed controls over a semester, and the less-skilled listeners gained most (Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari 2010, Language Learning 60(2)). Exam day

The final checklist

Read this the night before. Everything on it is a decision already made — on the day you only execute.

🌙The night before

  • No new vocabulary. Review your paraphrase journal and your trap list only.
  • Re-read your two personal mistakes from the top-mistakes slide.
  • Sleep. Listening performance is the most fatigue-sensitive part of the exam.
  • Pack: pencil(s), eraser, pen, candidate details, water.

⏱️On arrival

  • Sit as close to the front speakers as the invigilator allows. Position is a real advantage.
  • Check your audio is audible before the test starts. If not, raise your hand now.
  • Write your candidate number on the answer sheet carefully and check the digits.

📝During the paper

  • Always read the options in the silence. Never listen to an unread question.
  • Draft marks on hearing 1, firm circles on hearing 2. Never the same job twice.
  • Write only numbers and symbols in the margin.
  • If you lose an item, jump to the next question number immediately.
  • Never leave a blank. Guess, and move on.

✍️Transfer and finish

  • Blocks of five. Check each block. Cover the lines below with your hand.
  • Sweep for blanks, double-shaded lines, and the total count of 40.
  • Stop changing answers unless you have a clear reason. First instincts are usually right; panic edits are usually wrong.
The one-sentence version, if you remember nothing else: read before you listen, decide on the second hearing, distrust the words you recognise, and never leave a blank.

Key takeaways

Eight things to carry into the exam hall

If the rest of this workshop fades, keep these.

1Read before you listen

The silence before each extract is the only information you control. Never listen to an unread question.

2Two hearings, two jobs

Hearing 1 builds the map. Hearing 2 plants the flag. Never use both for the same task.

3Distrust the words you recognise

The key paraphrases; distractors quote. Recognition should make you suspicious, not comfortable.

4Hunt the pivot

But, however, actually, not, in fact, that said. The answer nearly always lives after the pivot.

5Ask who said it

Before “is it true?”, ask “who said it?”. The interviewer's voice is a trap factory.

6Symbols, not words

Numbers and symbols in the margin. Every word written while listening is a second not heard.

7Never leave a blank

A blank is a certain zero. Cambridge does not deduct marks for wrong MCQ answers — a four-option guess is worth 0.25 of a mark on average.

8Review beats repetition

One analysed paper outperforms three unexamined ones. Name the trap; never write “careless”.

The overall message: Paper 2 is not primarily a test of how much English you know. It is a test of how well you handle a recording you only hear twice. That is a skill — and skills improve with deliberate practice, not with hoping.
